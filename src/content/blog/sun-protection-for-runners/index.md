# Why Every Outdoor Race Needs a Sun-Safety Plan

*This is Part 7 of **Building a Digital-First Race** — a series on the digital and AI decisions behind the Mercedes-Benz Oakville Bronte Harbour Classic 5K, and why we made them. Last week covered the four-tool runner safety ecosystem; this one and the next two go deep on each piece, starting with sun.*

Read the race-day email from almost any local 5K and you'll find the same line, usually near the bottom, between "no headphones" and "no dogs": *don't forget your sunscreen.* That's the entire sun-safety plan at most outdoor races — a reminder, not a plan. Nobody is disputing that sunscreen helps. The problem is that sun protection for runners is a scheduling, logistics, and hydration decision as much as it's a skincare one, and most events never touch the first three.

> **Quick Answer:** A real sun-safety plan for an outdoor race sets start times to avoid peak UV, stations water and electrolytes where heat exposure is highest, briefs medical staff on heat-specific symptoms, and is honest about what it doesn't cover. We built ours around an 8:00/8:30 AM start, a course-appropriate water station, and an app I'd already built to solve the underlying math problem.

## The waterfront made this unavoidable

Bronte Heritage Waterfront Park is a beautiful place to run a 5K and a rough place to hide from the sun. The course runs along open water with very little tree cover, and the lake reflects UV back up at exactly the angle you'd design if you wanted to maximize exposure. I didn't plan the route with sun in mind — Charles Sathmary and I picked it because it's flat, scenic, and closes cleanly — but once the course was locked, the sun problem came with it, and it was on us to plan for it before Father's Day arrived.

That's the part most race organizers skip. A course gets chosen for logistics: road closures, parking, finish-line space, sponsor visibility. Sun exposure is rarely in the decision, and then it becomes the organizer's problem on race morning anyway, except now there's no time left to plan around it.

## The first decision was the schedule, not the gear

The single most effective sun-safety call we made cost nothing to implement: we scheduled the Kids 1K at 8:00 AM and the 5K at 8:30 AM. That's early for a Father's Day race — plenty of dads would rather sleep in — but it means the entire field is on the course and finished well before the UV index climbs toward its midday peak. The [EPA's UV Index scale](https://www.epa.gov/sunsafety/uv-index-scale-0) puts "high risk" starting around 6–7, and Oakville regularly sits in that range through a June morning before 9 AM. Push the start to 10 AM instead and you're racing through a meaningfully worse number for no operational reason.

This is the sun-safety decision that has nothing to do with apps, sunscreen, or hats. It's a scheduling choice, and it's available to any race director for free. The [Road Runners Club of America's event-director guidelines](https://www.rrca.org/education/event-directors/safe-event-guidelines/) make the same point from the medical side: heat and sun risk management starts with when you put people on the course, not with what you hand them at packet pickup.

## What sun protection for runners looks like once they're on course

Timing buys you a cooler morning; it doesn't solve exposure once 875 people are moving along open water for twenty to forty minutes. Two things are doing real work on course.

**Water and electrolytes, placed for the course, not just the distance.** The 5K has a staffed water station at the 2.5 km turnaround, plus water at the finish line. **[Natrilyte](https://www.bronteharbourclassic.com/news/welcome-natrilyte-hydration-sponsor/)**, our Official Hydration Partner, serves its electrolyte drink at both — 1,000 mg sodium and 400 mg magnesium per serving, formulated in an Oakville CrossFit gym specifically for people sweating hard outdoors. Heat and UV exposure both drain sodium faster than distance alone would predict, so this isn't just a fluid stop; it's a heat-load stop.

**Medical staff who know what heat looks like, not just what an injury looks like.** Trained first-aid volunteers and medical personnel are stationed at the start/finish area and along the course, with a physiotherapy and recovery tent at the festival grounds afterward. The [ACSM's guidance on exercising in hot environments](https://acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Exercising-in-hot-and-cold-environments.pdf) is specific about this: heat illness symptoms — confusion, stopped sweating, a runner who looks "fine" but is behaving oddly — get missed by staff trained only to spot twisted ankles and blisters. Briefing on that distinction is a five-minute conversation before race morning that most events skip entirely.

## Why I built the tool instead of just telling people to check the weather

Before I was co-directing a race, I was building sun-protection gear at GearTOP — hats, balaclavas, UV gloves — for hikers, anglers, and parents standing outside for hours at a stretch. Every one of them was making the same decision badly: how long can I actually stay out here? A raw UV number doesn't answer that. A UV Index of 7 means something different to someone with fair skin than to someone who tans easily, and it changes with cloud cover, time of day, and whether sunscreen is already on.

So I built **[SunUp](/projects/sunup/)** to answer the actual question instead of reporting the input to it: skin type, live UV index, cloud cover, time of day, and location go in; one number comes out — minutes until you burn. It's been live on iOS since September 2025, built the same way I build everything now: I specified the burn-time model, and AI closed the gap between knowing what the tool should do and having a working build to test.

I didn't build SunUp for the Bronte Harbour Classic — it existed first, for a completely different product line. The race turned out to be the best proof I have that the underlying problem is real: a course with no shade, a lake that bounces UV back at you, and hundreds of people who are not going to check an app mid-race no matter how good it is.

## What we haven't fixed yet

Here's the honest part. SunUp's burn-time model is built for one person deciding how long to stay on a beach or a trail — it isn't calibrated for a start corral of 875 people crossing an arch in the same three minutes, and I don't have a version of it that would meaningfully change what happens on race morning. We also didn't run a dedicated sunscreen station in 2026 — just guidance in the pre-race email to apply SPF before arriving, which is exactly the weak version of "sun safety" I opened this article criticizing. It's on the list for 2027, when the course adds a 10K and some runners will be exposed for close to twice as long.

I don't have injury or heat-incident data from June 21 that proves any of this worked better than doing nothing — a first-year race with zero heat-related medical incidents could just as easily mean we got lucky with the weather. What I can say is that we made every one of these decisions on purpose, before race day, instead of reacting to conditions once people were already on the course.

## What I'd tell another race director

If you're planning an outdoor event and sun hasn't come up yet, here's the order I'd work through it in, roughly in order of how cheap each fix is:

1. **Move the start time first.** It's free, and it's the single biggest lever you have.
2. **Look at your course for shade, not just for road closures.** If there isn't any, that's information, not a footnote.
3. **Put water and electrolytes where the exposure is worst**, not just at even distance intervals.
4. **Brief your medical team on heat symptoms specifically** — a five-minute conversation catches problems that generic first-aid training misses.
5. **Say what you didn't cover.** A sunscreen line in the race-day email is not a plan, and pretending it is one just means nobody fixes it next year.

None of this requires an app, a sponsor, or a budget line. It requires treating sun exposure as a course condition to design around, the same way you'd design around a hill or a narrow bridge.

## FAQ

### What does a sun-safety plan for an outdoor race actually include?

More than a sunscreen reminder in the race-day email. A real plan sets start times to avoid peak UV, places water and electrolyte stations where the course actually needs them, briefs medical staff on heat symptoms specifically, and gives an honest answer for what happens if conditions turn extreme. The Road Runners Club of America publishes event-director guidelines that cover most of this ground, and it is worth reading before your first race, not after a runner goes down.

### Why do the Kids 1K and 5K start so early in the morning?

Timing is the cheapest sun-safety decision a race director has. Our Kids 1K goes off at 8:00 AM and the 5K at 8:30 AM, which puts the entire field on the course before the UV index climbs toward its midday peak. It costs nothing to schedule a race two hours earlier. It is one of the most effective calls we made, and most of the credit goes to picking the time slot, not to anything we built.

### What is SunUp, and why did a race director build a UV app?

SunUp is a UV-safety app I built for GearTOP, my sun-protection product line, before I was directing a race. It takes skin type, live UV index, cloud cover, time of day, and location, and returns one number — minutes until you burn. It has been live on iOS since September 2025. I did not build it for the Bronte Harbour Classic; the race just turned out to be the best proof I have that the problem it solves is real.

### Does the water station on the course account for heat, not just distance?

Partly. The 5K has a staffed water station at the 2.5 km turnaround plus water at the finish, and Natrilyte — our Official Hydration Partner — serves an electrolyte drink formulated with 1,000 mg sodium and 400 mg magnesium per serving at both. That covers fluid and salt loss. It does not cover heat exposure itself, which is a separate problem station placement alone cannot solve.
